learning out loud · log 01

more dos than don'ts

walk 1 · step 1 of 6 · what a rule plants

a model reads your rule as words in its context. "never" is one word. the thing you're forbidding is all the others.

an LLM writes by predicting the next token from everything in its context: the system prompt, your skill file, the conversation. every word you put in there is material it can draw on. that's the whole trick, and it's also the trap.

when you write "never use bullet points," the words sitting in the context are use bullet points, with one small word in front asking it to do the opposite. usually it listens. but you spent your words describing the thing you don't want, and said nothing about what you do want.

pick a phrasing · see what lands in the context

anthropic's own prompting guide says the same thing in plain terms: tell the model what to do instead of what not to do. "write in flowing prose paragraphs" beats "don't use markdown." the "do" version describes the target. the "don't" version only rules out one of a thousand wrong answers.

walk 1 · step 2 of 6 · the ratio

so what's the ratio? let's scan a real rule file. mine.

i built a personal AI workout coach, and below are the first sentences of rules from its instruction file. of the 27 rules there, 17 opened with NEVER and 2 with ALWAYS. i wrote all of them. the meter sorts each line into three piles: a plain do, a don't with the do tucked in after it, and a don't with nothing to aim at.

walk 1 · step 3 of 6 · rewrite it

every don't has a do hiding behind it. find it first, then check yours.

the move is always the same question: if it didn't do the bad thing, what would it be doing instead? write that. keep the reason, because the reason is what lets a model handle the case your rule didn't list.

real rules · try it before you peek

the last card is the exception, and it's important. some don'ts should stay don'ts: the ones where the action is irreversible and high-stakes. sending an email, submitting a form, deleting a file. those get a hard wall. but even a wall works better with a do next to it, so the model knows where to stop and what to say when it gets there.

walk 1 · step 4 of 6 · where a rule should live

if you've written the same rule three times, the problem isn't the wording. it's where the rule lives.

here's the thing my coach taught me the hard way. a rule in prose is a suggestion the model usually follows. for most things, usually is plenty. for a fixed shape that has to be right every single time, usually means it breaks on a random tuesday. my blank workout log (the sheet i fill in by hand at the gym) broke seven times in three months, every time with the rule sitting right there in writing.

so rules climb a ladder. tap each rung.

the ladder · bottom is cheapest, top is strongest
tap a rung.

the skill file should be mostly rungs 2 and 3: dos with examples. rung 1 is for the handful of walls. and when something keeps breaking, you don't write a louder NEVER. you move it up to rung 4 or 5 and take it out of the model's hands.

walk 1 · step 5 of 6 · examples beat adjectives

"warm and casual" means something different to everyone. an example means one thing.

adjectives are the vaguest kind of do. "be concise," "sound warm," "make it professional." the model has to guess what you mean by them, and it guesses the average. an example removes the guessing. build up the instruction and watch how many different outputs it still allows.
